Measuring Turnaround in Medical Records Operations

WRITTEN BY Mari M
AUG 15 / 3 MIN READ

< THE QUESTION

Turnaround time sounds like a simple metric. A request goes out. Records come back. Count the days between the two.

Except a sent request is not necessarily a received request.

Several years ago, while supporting medical-record retrieval for a clinical trial patient recruitment program, I built an operational tracker to monitor requests, follow-up activity, pending records, and facility turnaround.

One metric that emerged—average turnaround time by facility—turned out to depend on a question that wasn't obvious at first: when does the clock actually start?

01

A SENT REQUEST IS NOT A RECEIVED REQUEST

The retrieval workflow contained an event between sending a release of information (ROI) and receiving records: confirmation that the facility had actually received the request.

MONDAY ROI SENT → TUESDAY NOT RECEIVED → WEDNESDAY RECEIPT CONFIRMED → MONDAY RECORDS RECEIVED

Suppose an ROI is sent Monday. Tuesday, the facility reports it was never received. It's resent, and receipt is confirmed Wednesday. Records arrive the following Monday.

What was the turnaround time? There are at least two reasonable answers:

RETRIEVAL CYCLE TIME

ROI SENT → RECORDS RECEIVED

This measures the elapsed time experienced by the requesting operation.

FACILITY PROCESSING TURNAROUND

ROI RECEIVED → RECORDS RECEIVED

This gets closer to measuring the time the facility actually had the request in hand.

Neither is inherently correct—they answer different questions.

Using the sent date to evaluate facility performance can attribute transmission failure or resubmission time to the facility's workflow. Using only confirmed receipt removes those delays from view, even though they still affect the total time to get the records.

<

THE CALCULATION IS EASY.
DEFINING WHAT IT REPRESENTS IS HARDER.

02

METRIC DEFINITION IS PROCESS DEFINITION

Choosing the start and end points of a turnaround metric doesn't just determine which dates go in a formula—it defines the boundaries of the process being measured.

That matters most in workflows that cross organizational boundaries, where a single number labeled "turnaround time" can quietly collapse transmission, facility intake, and processing into one interval. The resulting average is mathematically accurate and operationally ambiguous.

If the goal is the patient's total retrieval journey, sent date is the right start point. If the goal is comparing facility processing performance, confirmed receipt is more meaningful. If transmission failures are significant in their own right, they probably deserve their own metric rather than being absorbed into another one.

The first question isn't what dates do we have—it's what part of the workflow are we trying to understand.

03

FROM STATUS-BASED TO EXCEPTION-BASED MANAGEMENT

Once turnaround was defined and measured consistently, historical averages became useful for more than retrospective reporting—they gave context to active work.

A list of pending requests identifies unfinished work. It doesn't identify work that needs attention.

NORMAL

Three days pending at a facility that routinely takes five days.

EXCEPTION

Three days pending at a facility that normally returns records in one or two days.

That shifts the operational question from which requests are pending to which pending requests are behaving differently than expected.

Historical turnaround becomes an intervention threshold—a reference point for when normal waiting starts to look like an exception, rather than a reason to treat every open request as equally actionable.

04

WHEN DOWNSTREAM DATA CHANGES UPSTREAM DECISIONS

Over time, facility turnaround data started informing more than the retrieval queue.

During patient intake, the team would ask where a patient's records or prior testing were located—and facility identity could then be weighed against what was already known about that facility's retrieval difficulty.

That context mattered because it wasn't static. When recruitment needs required pursuing every potentially eligible patient, a difficult facility was simply a necessary dependency to work through.

When recruitment targets allowed more flexibility, expected retrieval difficulty could factor into how outreach effort was allocated.

A metric built to describe past facility performance had started to shape a real intake decision—not just report on retrieval after the fact.

A downstream operational metric had become an upstream input.

WHAT THE METRIC CAN DO

01

DESCRIBE PERFORMANCE

Establish historical turnaround patterns by facility.

02

IDENTIFY EXCEPTIONS

Surface pending requests behaving differently than expected.

03

PRIORITIZE FOLLOW-UP

Direct operational attention toward requests that actually warrant intervention.

04

INFORM UPSTREAM DECISIONS

Use historical retrieval difficulty as context during intake and outreach planning.

< CLOSING OBSERVATION

THE POINT

There's a difference between recording what happened and creating information that changes what happens next.

Facility turnaround started as a way to understand retrieval performance. Once patterns accumulated, it started identifying exceptions, prioritizing follow-up, and informing intake decisions it was never originally built for.

Healthcare operations generate enormous numbers of timestamps— referrals, authorizations, requests, deliveries, appointments, records received.

Calculating the difference between two of them is easy. The harder work is deciding which events actually define the process, what the resulting number attributes to each party in it, and whether it helps anyone make a better decision.

A useful metric should do more than count time. It should make the workflow easier to see.
